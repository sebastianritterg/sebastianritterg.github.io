  <article class="research-card research-card--featured">
    <div class="research-card__top"><div><h3>Too Far to Matter? The Urban Reach of Rural Territorial Conflict</h3><p class="research-card__byline">Sebastian Ritter · 2026</p></div><span class="research-card__status research-card__status--featured">Job market paper</span></div>
    <div class="research-card__grid">
      <div class="research-card__content">
        <p class="research-card__lede">Can rural territorial conflict reach nearby cities? Evidence from 104 urban areas in southern Chile links external conflict exposure to lower formal business activity.</p>
        <details class="research-card__abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>Can persistent territorial conflict affect nearby urban economies even when conflict events occur outside the city? I study this question in southern Chile, where the Mapuche self-determination conflict is recurrent and predominantly rural. I link geocoded attacks, coercive actions, and land invasions to a panel of 104 fixed urban areas and measure annual external exposure from events occurring within 25 kilometers but outside the receiving area. Greater external conflict exposure is associated with fewer registered enterprises and lower tax-reported firm sales. Within an urban area, an increase from the 25th to the 75th percentile of positive annual exposure is associated with about 2.2 percent fewer enterprises and 5.3 percent lower sales. First registrations also decline with exposure, pointing to weaker formal entry as an adjustment margin. Recorded terminations do not indicate greater exit. Complementary measures of nighttime lights and newly built area show no comparable contemporaneous decline. Fiscal appraisal also does not decline contemporaneously, but turns negative at medium lags. The formal-activity association is not systematically stronger where historical Títulos de Merced—the state titles that demarcated Mapuche reservations and now locate historically recognized territorial claims—cover more surrounding land, or where the baseline Mapuche population share is higher pointing to an association of the conflict itself and not to the Indigenous presence. The results show that the economic reach of recurrent territorial conflict can extend beyond contested land and into nearby urban economies, even in a setting where property, tax, Indigenous-land protection, and restitution institutions continue to operate.</p></details>
        <p class="research-card__metadata"><strong>Keywords</strong> Indigenous conflict · political violence · local economic activity · spatial spillovers · Chile</p>
        <p class="research-card__metadata"><strong>JEL</strong> D74 · O18 · R11</p>

    <div class="research-card__top"><div><h3>The Geography of the Green Transition: Performance, Vulnerabilities and Opportunities</h3><p class="research-card__byline">Sebastian Ritter and Vicente Royuela · <em>Papers in Regional Science</em>, 2026</p></div><span class="research-card__status">Published</span></div>
    <div class="research-card__grid">
      <div class="research-card__content">
        <p class="research-card__lede">A new index tracks green-transition performance across 232 European regions and places that performance alongside regional opportunities and vulnerabilities.</p>
        <details class="research-card__abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>The green transition is reshaping Europe's development geography, yet progress remains uneven across regions. Effective policy requires systematic tools to monitor how territories perform, adapt, and benefit from this transformation. This paper proposes a practical and multidimensional definition of the green transition aligned with the European Commission's strategic directions and introduces a new Regional Green Transition Performance Index covering 232 NUTS-2 regions from 2009 to 2022. The index measures progress across seven pillars - environment, energy, circular economy, sustainable development, just transition, innovation and policy, and mobility - offering the first harmonized and longitudinal framework to track regional transition performance. Beyond performance, we introduce the Regional Green Transition Opportunity Index, capturing regional assets and capacities that enable adaptation and growth, and combine it with the existing Regional Green Transition Vulnerability Index. This yields an opportunity-vulnerability typology distinguishing resilient frontrunners, missed potentials, stable but stagnant, and at-risk regions. Methodologically, we conduct extensive robustness tests in the construction of the composite indices and estimate a battery of econometric convergence models to explore spatial dynamics. Results reveal significant spatial dependence and convergence in green transition performance, highlighting territorial interdependencies across regional systems. By integrating multidimensional performance with structural vulnerabilities and opportunities, the paper provides policymakers with an evidence-based, region-level monitoring tool to design balanced, place-sensitive strategies that both mitigate risks and mobilize opportunities for a just and cohesive European green transition.</p></details>
        <p class="research-card__metadata"><strong>Keywords</strong> Green transition · European Union · regional inequality · green opportunities</p>

    <div class="research-card__top"><div><h3>The Economic Effect of Splitting a Region in a Centralized Country: A Case from Chile</h3><p class="research-card__byline">Sebastian Ritter · <em>Global Challenges &amp; Regional Science</em>, 2025</p></div><span class="research-card__status">Published</span></div>
    <div class="research-card__grid">
      <div class="research-card__content">
        <p class="research-card__lede">Chile’s 2007 creation of two regions offers evidence on territorial reform, growth, and the value of administrative status.</p>
        <details class="research-card__abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>The economic impact of territorial reforms has been discussed in the literature but mostly looking at amalgamations rather than de-amalgamations. Little is known about the impact of territorial splits and even less in the context of a centralized and emerging country. This paper addresses this gap by analyzing the 2007 territorial reform in Chile, where two new regions were created at opposite ends of the country. By using a synthetic control approach, a difference-in-differences and event studies, the causal effect of such reform over GDP and private sales on the affected regions is estimated, including the effect of the new administrative status for the cities of Valdivia, La Union, Arica and Putre. After accounting for all potential external shocks, no significant impact of the division on the affected regions was found. However, at the municipal level, while regional capital status had no significant effect, transitioning from a regular municipality to a provincial capital had a notable positive impact in a small, low-urbanization city such as La Union. We believe these results are very important as they illustrate that policies aimed at splitting regions or relocating the capital status to promote growth should consider the context and the scope of competences of the subnational governments as well as the socioeconomic characteristics of the places.</p></details>
        <p class="research-card__metadata"><strong>Keywords</strong> Territorial reform · synthetic control · decentralization · local governments · administrative status</p>

    <div class="research-card__top"><div><h3>Will the Green Transition Deepen Regional Divides? Evidence from Mobility Intentions across European Regions</h3><p class="research-card__byline">With Vicente Royuela and Yann Marx</p></div><span class="research-card__status research-card__status--wip">Under review</span></div>
    <div class="research-card__grid">
      <div class="research-card__content">
        <p class="research-card__lede">Survey evidence connects green preferences and regional green opportunities to Europeans’ stated intentions to move.</p>
        <details class="research-card__abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>The green transition remains at the core of current policy agendas as societies respond to the threats associated with climate change. Previous research has pointed to the possible distributional consequences of the green transition, but much of this discussion remains closer to hypothetical scenarios than to empirical evidence. This paper examines whether Europe's green transition is associated with mobility intentions that may amplify regional inequality. We link MOBI-TWIN microdata on intentions to move (by time horizon) along with respondents' green preferences to NUTS2 indicators of regional green transition performance, opportunities, and vulnerability, alongside standard regional controls. Multilevel logit estimates indicate that, among respondents who report an intended destination, opportunity-rich green regions are more likely to be reported, and this association is stronger at longer time horizons. Green preferences are a strong individual predictor of intending to move and are associated with modest differences in the regional profiles of stated long-run destinations: greener-performing destinations are more often reported by highly pro-environmental individuals, while vulnerable destinations are less often reported by this group. The results suggest that an uneven distribution of green opportunities may translate into unequal future population dynamics across regions. From a policy perspective, this points to the need to complement risk-compensation with place-based capability building and the diffusion of green opportunities in lagging regions, so that the transition supports cohesion rather than divergence.</p></details>
        <p class="research-card__metadata"><strong>Keywords</strong> Green transition · mobility intentions · regional inequality · environmental preferences · EU regions</p>

    <div class="research-card__top"><div><h3>Do Language Policies Affect Internal Migration in a Multilingual Country?</h3><p class="research-card__byline">With Antonio Di Paolo, Raül Ramos, and Vicente Royuela</p></div><span class="research-card__status research-card__status--wip">In progress</span></div>
    <div class="research-card__grid">
      <div class="research-card__content">
        <p class="research-card__lede">Catalonia’s language-normalization reforms provide a setting to study whether language policy can alter internal migration.</p>
        <details class="research-card__abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper studies whether a regional language policy can alter internal migration within a multilingual country. We examine the Catalan language-normalization policy sequence that began with education and public-administration changes in the early 1980s and culminated in the 1983 Language Normalization Act. Using annual administrative data on interregional migration flows for 1968-1999, we estimate the effect of this reform sequence with a set of synthetic-control methods techniques. The aggregate evidence points to a decline in migration inflows to Catalonia following the onset of the reform sequence, with effects already visible around 1981. We then use retrospective residential histories from the 1991 Spanish Socio-Demographic Survey to provide suggestive evidence on mechanisms. The microdata shows stronger declines in destination choice among families with school-age children and among teachers, consistent with the educational and public-sector components of the policy. Overall, the paper provides quasi-experimental evidence that a broad regional language policy can impose unintended cost by altering spatial mobility.</p></details>
        <p class="research-card__metadata"><strong>Keywords</strong> Language policy · internal migration · synthetic control · Catalonia</p>
